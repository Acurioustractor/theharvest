## Body — paste into the article editor

There is a London museum with half of a Witta tree in it.

In 1908, a red cedar from Frank Dunlop's selection up at Maleny — six metres twenty-seven around the trunk — was hauled up Walker's Pinch by a combined team of sixty bullocks. It went to the Franco-British Exhibition in London and won a prize. Half the log is reportedly still over there.

Twenty-two years before that, Isaac Burgess had sent two even larger cedars to the Indian and Colonial Exhibition. They couldn't be sold. No mill in the world could process logs that big.

The first Europeans on the Blackall Range called cedar **red gold**. Aboriginal people knew it as *Wootha*. By 1906, twenty years after Burgess's logs went unsold in London, red cedar faced commercial extinction. Eighteen years after Dunlop's prize log, the Mapleton Tramway closed. The trees that built the tramway, that floated down the chutes carved into the range, that crossed Pumicestone Passage in chained rafts — they were gone.

### What's growing now

The trees on the range today aren't the giants. They are second-growth and third-growth. Spared, missed, regrown. They have the same DNA as the trees that went to London. They just haven't had the time.

Some of them are on Barry Rodgerig's property, a few hills over from The Harvest.

Barry has been running a nursery in this hinterland for twenty-seven years. He has spent that time nursing what was left. He knows which trees came back from stumps and which seeded new. He knows which boards check and which stay flat. He knows what's safe to cut and what to leave.

We are building The Harvest with timber from his land.

### What we are making with it

The cedar (and some hoop pine, also from local properties) goes into the pieces of The Harvest that hands touch:

- **Bench seats** under the Milk Create Pavilion
- **The bar** in the kitchen
- **The gallery rail** in the art space
- **The kitchen pass** between the back kitchen and the long table
- **The shop's** shelving when we build it

We use it unfinished where we can. The grain stays visible. Where a knot or a split tells a story, we keep it. A board with a mill mark across it keeps the mill mark across it.

### Why this matters

Choosing local cedar over imported plantation hardwood is a small act. Logistically it's easier the other way. Hardwood from elsewhere is straighter, more uniform, cheaper to truck.

But the range was stripped of cedar inside two generations. The act of working with what came back, on the land where it almost ended, with the people who watched it almost end, is the act we wanted to do. It costs more in dressing time and care. It returns more in legibility.

Every plank in The Harvest is a quiet refusal of the 1906 ending.

### What's next

This year we mill enough for the first round of work — pavilion benches, gallery rail, kitchen pass. Next year we mill for the sauna we are planning, which will be cedar-lined. After that we will be working with hoop pine for the shop fittings.

If you have a tree on your land you have been thinking about milling, talk to Barry. We may be able to use it. The pieces it ends up in will have a card with your property's name on it.
