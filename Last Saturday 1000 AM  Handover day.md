### **Video Summary**

This BBC Arts documentary, released to mark the centenary of the Bauhaus, traces the school's tumultuous 14-year history (1919–1933) and its profound influence on modern design. It follows the school's journey through three German cities—Weimar, Dessau, and Berlin—under three different directors, highlighting how political instability, radical creativity, and the rise of Nazism shaped its existence and eventual diaspora.

### **Detailed Timeline of Events**

**1. Origins and the Weimar Years (1919–1925)**

- **Founding Vision:** The Bauhaus was founded in Weimar by architect **Walter Gropius** shortly after WWI. deeply affected by the horrors of the war, Gropius wanted to "redesign society" and use technology constructively rather than destructively **[[07:11](http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wwYZQjgUljA&t=431)]**. He merged the School of Applied Arts with the School of Fine Arts, aiming to erase the barrier between the artist and the craftsman **[[08:15](http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wwYZQjgUljA&t=495)]**.
- **The Manifesto:** Gropius issued a manifesto calling for a "new building of the future" that would unite architecture, sculpture, and painting **[[10:46](http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wwYZQjgUljA&t=646)]**. This attracted a diverse group of students, including war veterans and young radicals.
- **The Preliminary Course:** A key innovation was the "Preliminary Course" (Vorkurs) led by **Johannes Itten**, an eccentric teacher who followed the Mazdaznan cult. He taught students to "unlearn" their habits, using breathing exercises and studying the "nature" of materials **[[16:29](http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wwYZQjgUljA&t=989)]**.
- **Gender Inequality:** Despite Gropius's claim of equality ("51% women in the first semester"), the school practiced gender segregation. Women were largely forced into the **Weaving Workshop** rather than architecture or metalworking, though figures like **Marianne Brandt** eventually broke into the male-dominated Metal Workshop **[[24:16](http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wwYZQjgUljA&t=1456)]**.
- **Conflict with Locals:** The conservative citizens of Weimar viewed the "hippie" students as immoral weirdos. Tensions rose, leading the government to cut funding. In a desperate bid to survive, the school held a massive exhibition in 1923 (the "Haus am Horn"), but it failed to win over the locals **[[31:30](http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wwYZQjgUljA&t=1890)]**.

**2. The Dessau Golden Age (1925–1928)**

- **Relocation:** Driven out of Weimar by right-wing nationalists, the school moved to the industrial city of **Dessau** in 1925. Here, Gropius designed the iconic **Bauhaus building**—a glass and steel structure that symbolized the school's ideals **[[34:45](http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wwYZQjgUljA&t=2085)]**.
- **Life at Dessau:** This period is considered the school's "golden age." It was a time of immense creativity and legendary themed parties (e.g., the Metal Party). The masters lived in purpose-built houses nearby.
- **Gropius Resigns:** Exhausted by constant political battles and attacks on his character, Gropius resigned in 1928 to focus on his private architectural practice **[[39:14](http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wwYZQjgUljA&t=2354)]**.

**3. Hannes Meyer and Functionalism (1928–1930)**

- **New Direction:** Gropius appointed the Swiss Marxist architect **Hannes Meyer** as his successor. Meyer shifted the focus away from aesthetics toward strict functionalism and social purpose, coining the phrase "needs of the people instead of luxury needs" **[[40:31](http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wwYZQjgUljA&t=2431)]**.
- **Commercial Success:** Ironically, under the communist Meyer, the school became its most commercially successful, largely due to the sales of **Bauhaus Wallpaper** **[[41:35](http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wwYZQjgUljA&t=2495)]**.
- **Dismissal:** Meyer's open Marxist politics and the increasing radicalization of the students alienated the local authorities. As the Nazis gained power, Meyer was fired in 1930 to protect the school **[[43:55](http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wwYZQjgUljA&t=2635)]**.

**4. Mies van der Rohe and the End (1930–1933)**

- **Authoritarian Leadership:** The final director was the renowned architect **Ludwig Mies van der Rohe**. He transformed the Bauhaus into a strict school of architecture and attempted to de-politicize it, expelling communist students and banning political activity **[[45:08](http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wwYZQjgUljA&t=2708)]**.
- **Closure:** In 1932, the Nazis took control of the Dessau city council and closed the school. Mies moved the operation to an abandoned telephone factory in **Berlin**, operating as a private institution **[[46:45](http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wwYZQjgUljA&t=2805)]**.
- **The Final Days:** In 1933, after the Gestapo raided the school, the faculty voted to dissolve the Bauhaus rather than submit to Nazi ideology. They famously drank a bottle of champagne and went their separate ways **[[48:20](http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wwYZQjgUljA&t=2900)]**.

### **Legacy and Diaspora**

- **Spread to America:** The forced closure ironically helped spread Bauhaus ideas globally. Key figures fled to the US: Gropius went to **Harvard**, Mies to **IIT in Chicago**, and Josef and Anni Albers to **Black Mountain College** **[[49:36](http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wwYZQjgUljA&t=2976)]**.

Here are the most significant movements similar to the Bauhaus:

### **1. Vkhutemas (The "Soviet Bauhaus")**

- **Where:** Moscow, Russia (1920–1930)
- **The Vibe:** Radical, industrial, revolutionary.

### **2. De Stijl (The Style)**

- **Where:** The Netherlands (1917–1931)
- **The Vibe:** Mathematical, pure, abstract.
- **The Look:** Think of a Piet Mondrian painting turned into a house. White walls, black lines, and rectangles of primary colors (red, blue, yellow).
- **Why it’s like Bauhaus:** It wasn't a school, but a tight-knit collective of artists and architects who believed that "pure" geometry could create harmony in society. Bauhaus director Walter Gropius was heavily influenced by them.
- **Key Building:** The **Rietveld Schröder House** in Utrecht is the perfect embodiment of this manifesto.

### **3. The Arts and Crafts Movement**

- **Where:** UK (Late 19th Century) → Spread to USA
- **The Vibe:** Anti-industrial, romantic, handmade.
- **Why it’s like Bauhaus:** This was the "grandfather" of the Bauhaus. Led by **William Morris**, it believed that bad architecture and cheap factory goods made people unhappy. They wanted to return to the "honest" craftsmanship of the Middle Ages.
- **The Twist:** The Bauhaus started with these ideals but later embraced machines; Arts & Crafts hated machines to the end.
- **Key Legacy:** It heavily influenced the "Craftsman" and "Bungalow" styles seen all over American suburbs.

### **4. Taliesin / Organic Architecture**

- **Where:** USA (Wisconsin & Arizona)
- **The Leader:** **Frank Lloyd Wright**
- **The Vibe:** Harmony with nature, horizontal lines, cult-like apprenticeship.
- **Why it’s like Bauhaus:** Wright founded the **Taliesin Fellowship**, which was a live-in architecture school similar to the Bauhaus. Students (apprentices) lived, farmed, cooked, and built together in a community.
- **The Philosophy:** "Organic Architecture"—buildings should grow out of the land, not sit on top of it (e.g., **Fallingwater**).

### **5. Metabolism**

- **Where:** Japan (1960s)
- **The Vibe:** Biological, futuristic, sci-fi.
- **Why it’s like Bauhaus:** A group of young Japanese architects published a manifesto proposing that buildings shouldn't be static; they should be like living organisms that grow and change cells. It was a direct response to the destruction of WWII, aimed at rebuilding society.
- **Key Building:** The **Nakagin Capsule Tower** in Tokyo, which consisted of replaceable concrete "pods" plugged into a central core.

### **6. Brutalism**

- **Where:** Global (1950s–1970s)
- **The Vibe:** Raw concrete, massive scales, defensive.
- **Why it’s like Bauhaus:** It grew directly out of the Bauhaus diaspora (specifically Le Corbusier and Mies van der Rohe's influence). It was driven by a socialist utopian ideal: providing affordable, honest, and functional housing for the masses.
- **The Result:** It created the massive concrete government buildings and housing estates found from London to Washington D.C. to Chandigarh, India. Like the Bauhaus, it eventually faced a massive public backlash.

The success of the Bauhaus in drawing people in—much like the rave scenes or music cults you mentioned—relied on the fact that it offered **salvation, not just education**.

After the horror of World War I, young people were traumatized and disillusioned with the "old world." They didn't just want to learn how to paint; they wanted to learn how to live. Walter Gropius understood this, and he designed the Bauhaus to be a total spiritual and philosophical reboot of society.

Here is how the manifesto and the "cult" atmosphere merged art and philosophy to create a magnetic pull.

### **1. The Manifesto: The Call to Arms**

Most schools start with a syllabus. The Bauhaus started with a manifesto that read like a religious prophecy.

- **The Hook:** Gropius didn't talk about "industrial design" or "profit." He talked about "the crystal symbol of a new faith." He framed the artist not as a decorator, but as a crucial figure in saving society.
- **The Symbol:** The cover of the manifesto wasn't a building; it was a woodcut of a "Cathedral of Socialism" shining with stars. It signaled to young radicals that this was a spiritual mission.

**Why it drew people in:** It promised **purpose**. It told lost, post-war youth that if they came to Weimar, they wouldn't just be students; they would be the builders of a new civilization.

### **2. The "Cult": The Mazdaznan Phase**

You mentioned "cult movements," and the early Bauhaus (1919–1923) was literally a cult. This was largely due to **Johannes Itten**, the teacher of the Preliminary Course.

- **The Look:** Itten shaved his head, wore monk-like robes, and smelled of garlic (which he ate in vast quantities to "purify" himself).
- **The Rituals:** Before touching a pencil, students had to perform breathing exercises and gymnastics to "tune" their bodies.
- **The Philosophy:** Itten followed **Mazdaznan**, a fire-worshiping cult. He taught that "intuition" was more important than "method." He forced students to "unlearn" everything they knew about art to find their inner child.

**Why it drew people in:** It created an **exclusive identity**. Just like wearing the right clothes at the Haçienda or being a "follower" of the Brian Jonestown Massacre, the garlic breath and shaved heads marked you as an "insider." It created a tight-knit "us vs. them" mentality that is addictive for young creatives.

### **3. The Structure: The Curriculum Wheel**

The brilliance of the Bauhaus was that they didn't keep Art and Philosophy separate. They mashed them together into a single diagram: The Curriculum Wheel.

- **The Outer Ring (Vorkurs/Preliminary Course):** This was the gatekeeper. Every student, whether they wanted to be an architect or a weaver, had to take this 6-month course on form, color, and materials. It was pure theory and philosophy—brainwashing (in a positive sense) them into the Bauhaus way of thinking.
- **The Middle Rings (Workshops):** Only after you were "purified" could you enter the workshops (Metal, Wood, Glass). Here, philosophy met the machine. You weren't just making a chair; you were applying the "truth" of materials to a functional object.
- **The Center (Building):** The ultimate goal was "The Building"—the total work of art (Gesamtkunstwerk) where all skills combined.

**Why it drew people in:** It offered a **clear path**. It wasn't messy or random. It showed you exactly how you would progress from an apprentice to a master, integrating your soul (art) with reality (craft).
