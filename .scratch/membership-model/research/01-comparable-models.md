# Comparable membership models worldwide

Research ticket: `.scratch/membership-model/issues/01-comparable-models-worldwide.md`
Written: 2026-07-29. All URLs accessed 2026-07-29 unless stated.

## How to read this

Every price below is quoted as the organisation states it, then converted. Conversions use the
ECB reference rates published for 2026-07-28 via the Frankfurter API
(https://api.frankfurter.dev/v1/latest?base=AUD, accessed 2026-07-29):
1 AUD = 0.52446 GBP = 0.61305 EUR = 0.69685 USD. So GBP 1 is about AUD 1.91, EUR 1 is about
AUD 1.63, USD 1 is about AUD 1.44. Converted figures are rounded and marked "approx".

Where a figure could not be sourced from the organisation itself, it says so. Nothing here is
estimated. Claims taken from trade press or a third party are labelled **secondary**.

The year attached to each price is the year the page states, or the year the page was
accessed if the page carries no date. Most of these pages carry a 2026 copyright line and
current pricing, so most figures are 2026 figures.

## Part 1: The organisations

### 1. Community Sauna Baths, London (UK)

The closest single analogue in the set for the sauna question, though urban not rural. A
not-for-profit Community Interest Company running five outdoor sauna sites.

**Tiers.** Three paid memberships, no free tier, plus a separate and quite different
concession system.

| Tier | Price | Approx AUD | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| Regulars Club | GBP 23/month or GBP 230/year | approx A$44/mo, A$439/yr | 50% off every session peak and off-peak, priority member slots, 15% off private hire, member-only events |
| Unlimited Off-Peak | GBP 35/month or GBP 350/year | approx A$67/mo, A$667/yr | Unlimited sessions Mon to Fri 7am to 4pm, capped at two bookings a day |
| The Full Steam | GBP 55/month or GBP 550/year | approx A$105/mo, A$1,049/yr | Both of the above combined |

Source: https://www.community-sauna.co.uk/regulars-join

**What is gated.** Nothing is free. Everything is a paid session; membership only changes the
price and the booking priority. Walk-in sessions start at GBP 9.50 (approx A$18) for weekday
mornings, per the tier pages and third-party listings.

**Concessions, which are the interesting part.** They run a parallel set of free-to-hold
"concession memberships" that unlock a cheaper *ticket price* rather than free access:

- General Concessions: students, people on Universal Credit, tax credits, jobseekers
  allowance or housing benefit, and over-65s. Valid 6 months. Gets GBP 5 off-peak sauna
  (approx A$9.50). Requires photo ID plus a benefit statement dated within three months.
- **Stratford Locals**: "Anyone with proof of living in E15 and E20". Valid 6 months.
  GBP 5 off-peak, GBP 11 peak. Requires proof of address plus photo ID.
- **Walthamstow Locals**: anyone living on two named streets adjacent to the site gets 20% off.
  Valid 1 year.
- Disability concession: valid 1 year, and "Includes free access for a carer or companion".
- NHS and Blue Light: GBP 2 per month (approx A$3.80), gets GBP 5 off-peak plus two free NHS
  slots per month.
- A "2 for 1" arrangement for parents who alternate childcare: buy one ticket, two people
  share the session by swapping.

Source: https://www.community-sauna.co.uk/concessions

**Rationing.** Two mechanisms. Peak vs off-peak splits demand by time, and the cheapest
unlimited tier is off-peak only with a hard cap of two bookings per day. "Priority member
slots" means some booking slots are held back for members. One site, Peckham, is excluded
from all memberships.

**Verbatim wording worth stealing.**

> "At Community Sauna Baths, we believe that access to warmth, rest and meaningful connection
> shouldn't depend on your financial situation."

> "All profit goes back into the community, covering free and low-cost sessions for NHS
> workers, low-income local residents, and grassroots groups. It also helps us maintain and
> grow our sites, run events at cost..."

Their tier names are written as descriptions of a person, not a product: "For the one who
makes the most of the quieter hours", "For the one who knows all the staff (and cats) by
name", "For the one who is fully sauna obsessed, (we are obsessed with you too)".

Source: https://www.community-sauna.co.uk/aboutpage and the memberships page above.

**Presentation.** Story leads on the About page, price leads on the memberships page. The two
are held together by the impact numbers they publish on the About page: 7,500+ free NHS
sessions, 3,600+ concession members, 700+ people supported through social prescribing,
1,500+ members using regular sessions. Note the shape of that: the concession cohort is more
than twice the paying member cohort. The community claim is made credible by volume of
subsidised access, not by anything being free to everyone.

**Failures or walk-backs.** None found. One thing visible in the pricing: their own summer
promotion says members joining in summer 2026 "Lock in GBP 35/month", which implies a price
rise for later joiners. A third-party listing reports the post-September price as GBP 45;
I could not confirm that on their own site, so treat it as **secondary and unverified**.

### 2. Blue Mountains Sauna, Leura NSW (Australia)

Australia's benchmark for what a sauna visit costs here. Semi-rural, established 2022, a Pty
Ltd rather than a not-for-profit. Directly relevant because it sets the price a Sunshine Coast
visitor will compare against.

**Casual prices (2026, AUD, native currency):** single pass A$45 off-peak, A$55 peak. Packs
scale down: 10-pack A$400 off-peak / A$525 peak; 40-pack A$1,199 off-peak / A$1,880 peak.
Off-peak is Mon to Fri 7am to 9pm plus weekends 7am to 10am.

**Memberships, priced by weekly session allowance:**

| Plan | Allowance | Price |
|---|---|---|
| Rise & Sweat | Two 1-hour sessions/week, Mon to Fri | A$32/week |
| Balance | 2 sessions/week | A$56/week |
| Renew | 3 sessions/week | A$66/week |
| Restore | 4 sessions/week | A$76/week |
| Revive | 7 sessions/week | A$91/week |

Source: https://bmsauna.punchpass.com/catalogs

**Rationing.** Explicit and numeric: the membership *is* an allowance of sessions per week,
enforced by the booking system (Punchpass). Peak/off-peak splits demand. Aufguss ritual
sessions are included but capacity-limited to one per booked session. Private group hire is
sold separately at A$1,150 to A$1,650 for two hours.

**Presentation.** Story leads hard. The homepage runs a ticker of claims about culture, not
price: "PUBLIC SAUNA. NOT A SPA.", "ALL AGES, ALL GENDERS, ALL POSTCODES", "IN THE SAUNA
EVERYONE IS EQUAL", "Spread the good heat". Pricing sits behind a "PASSES" link. Their
etiquette copy is worth reading in full for tone; it is written as house rules, not terms:

> "Conversation is welcome, but keep it calm and low. The sauna is not a pub, if it can be
> heard across the room, it's too loud."

> "This is a neutral space. Leave divisive topics, politics, religion, vaccinations, gender
> debates, at the door."

Source: https://bmsauna.com.au/ and https://bmsauna.com.au/passes

**Concessions.** None published. This is a commercial operator with a community tone, and no
low-income rate appears anywhere on the site or in the Punchpass catalogue.

**Decision-relevant.** A sauna visit in Australia is a A$45 thing. Any Harvest model that
makes the sauna a free member perk is giving away roughly A$45 of retail value per visit.
At even one visit a fortnight that is over A$1,100 a year of foregone revenue per member.

### 3. Sompasauna, Helsinki (Finland)

The pure free-and-volunteer end of the spectrum, and the reason to take that end seriously.

Free, open 24/7, no staff, no booking, no membership. Users heat the stove themselves, fetch
their own water, and are expected to chop wood and contribute. The one rule is *saunarauha*,
sauna peace. Two wood-fired saunas with sea access.

The primary write-up I could find is a Shareable interview with the volunteer organisers:
https://www.shareable.net/helsinki-volunteers-reinvent-the-public-sauna/ . The rest of my
detail comes from **secondary** travel and sauna-press sources
(https://www.saunatimes.com/types-of-saunas/public-saunas/sompasauna-moves-from-social-experiment-to-social-proof/,
https://atlasobscura.com/places/sompasauna). I did not find an official pricing or governance
page, because there is not one.

**What this shows.** A sauna can be genuinely free and genuinely maintained, but only if the
labour of running it is transferred to the users at the point of use. The absence of staff is
not incidental to the free price, it *is* the free price. It also means no bookings, no
supervision, no guaranteed temperature and, importantly for The Harvest, a liability posture
no Australian operator with a lease and a public liability policy could adopt.

**Contrast for the same city:** Loyly Helsinki charges EUR 29 for a two-hour public sauna
visit, plus EUR 12 per additional hour (approx A$47 and A$20). Source:
https://www.loylyhelsinki.fi/en/public-sauna . So within one city, the same activity ranges
from free-and-self-run to A$47-a-visit-and-staffed. The variable is who does the work.

### 4. CERES Community Environment Park, Brunswick East (Australia)

The single most transferable model in this set, because the shape is almost identical to The
Harvest: a free-to-enter public park containing a garden, an urban farm, trading enterprises,
a venue for hire and an education programme.

**Tiers.** Two, both paid, no free tier and no free-tier equivalent, because the *place* is
the free tier.

- Concession Member: A$30 per year
- Individual Member: A$66 per year

Source: https://ceres.org.au/membership/ (2026)

**What is gated.** Essentially nothing physical. Membership buys a 10% discount at CERES
Brunswick East enterprises (nursery and bookstore, grocery and bakery, cafe, venue hire,
visitor centre, workshops and courses) plus voting rights and a say in strategic direction.
Commercial venue hire is excluded, as are several grocery categories sold near cost.

Their own framing of the free/paid boundary is the sentence they repeat in the site header on
every page:

> "CERES is a public park that is free to visit. Enjoy the green spaces, the community, and
> our enterprises."

And the membership pitch leads with belonging, not benefit:

> "When you join CERES as a member, you're joining a community of people that care about each
> other, and the Earth."

**Admin load.** Low but not zero. New members are subject to approval by the CERES Board at
its next regular meeting, though "Discounts apply immediately upon signing up and are not
subject to Board approval". That split, immediate commercial benefit, deferred governance
admission, is a neat piece of design worth copying if The Harvest ever gives members a vote.

**Presentation.** Price follows the story, but only just. The membership page is short: one
paragraph of why, a benefits list, two price cards, then the constitutional fine print.

### 5. Northey Street City Farm, Brisbane (Australia)

The nearest geographic and structural comparator, an incorporated association running an urban
permaculture farm, nursery, market and education programme.

**Membership tiers, self-declared by employment status.** No means test, no documentation.

| Tier | Annual fee (AUD, 2026) |
|---|---|
| Health Care Card holders | $15 |
| Part-time employees | $30 |
| Full-time employees | $60 |
| Deep Roots Supporter | $125 |
| Community organisation (non-profit, non-govt) | $75 |
| Company / corporate | $250 |

Source: https://www.nscf.org.au/become-a-member-of-northey-street-city-farm/

**What membership buys.** 10% off plants and merchandise, 5% off the Permaculture Design
Course, library borrowing rights, monthly enews, early-bird event invitations, an introductory
seed packet, AGM voting rights and eligibility for the management committee. Nothing about
access to the land: the farm itself is open.

**Separately, the allotment gardens are a rental, not a membership benefit.** Beds are 1m x
4.5m. Annual fee: A$208 concession / A$312 general, plus A$130 concession / A$156 general for
an irrigated bed, plus a A$70 refundable bond. Membership is explicitly optional for allotment
holders: "We encourage allotment holders to become NSCF Members, though this is optional."
A number of allotments are free of charge for First Nations people.

Source: https://www.nscf.org.au/allotment-gardens/

**Why this matters for The Harvest.** Northey Street cleanly separates three things The
Harvest is currently tempted to bundle: (a) a cheap supporter membership that gates nothing,
(b) a per-unit rental for a scarce physical asset, priced at cost recovery with a concession
rate, and (c) free access to the land for everyone. The scarce asset is not a membership perk.
It is its own product with its own price.

The concession rate is set by self-declaration for membership, and by producing a card for the
allotment ("For concession card holders, we ask that you provide a copy of your concession
card"). Documentation is required only where real money is at stake.

### 6. Brisbane Tool Library (Australia)

**Tiers.** Four, all paid, differing on borrowing capacity and duration, not on access.

| Tier | Price (AUD, 2026) | Borrow limit | Workshops |
|---|---|---|---|
| Starter (3 months) | $85 | 6 items per loan | standard price |
| Concession (annual) | $95 | 6 items per loan | 10% off |
| Regular (annual) | $110 | 6 items per loan | 10% off |
| Lover (annual) | $125 | 12 items per loan | 10% off |

Source: https://www.brisbanetoollibrary.org/memberships

**Rationing.** Item count per loan, a fixed two-week loan period with an optional one-week
renewal "if no-one else is in need of the item at that time", and reservations handled by
off-the-shelf software (MyTurn). Note the low admin: the queue is the rationing mechanism, and
the software runs it.

**Presentation.** The headline is a price comparison, not a story: "Whether you are an avid
builder, a part time tinkerer or a curious beginner, we have a membership to suit you, all for
less than the cost of one drill!" That is the sharpest one-line justification of an annual fee
in this whole set. It answers "why pay" with a substitution the reader can check themselves.

They also sell gift memberships, and say plainly why concessions exist: "Concessions and gift
memberships available, we want everyone to be able to access to our inventory." (sic)

**A caution on tier spacing.** Concession $95, Regular $110, Lover $125. The gap between
concession and regular is $15 a year. That is close enough that the concession tier is
effectively a self-declared nudge rather than a real affordability measure, and the three-month
Starter at $85 is worse value than a full year at any other tier. Worth avoiding.

### 7. Australian Men's Sheds

A useful floor price for "membership of a shared workshop in an Australian country town".
Individual sheds set their own fees. Verified individual examples were not all reachable
directly; the figures below come from a search summary of the sheds' own membership pages and
should be treated as **partly secondary** until each page is opened:

- Sorell Men's Shed (Tas): $60/year (https://www.sorellmensshed.org.au/membership/)
- Buderim Men's Shed (Qld, Sunshine Coast): $60/year (https://buderimmensshed.org/membership/,
  returned HTTP 406 to my fetch, so **unverified directly**)
- Bentmoor Men's Shed: $60/year
- Wynnum Manly & District Men's Shed: $70 from 1 April 2024
- Subiaco Community Men's Shed (WA): $130/year

Australian Men's Shed Association membership for the sheds themselves is free to eligible
groups (https://mensshed.org/for-mens-sheds/join-amsa/). Victorian Men's Shed Association
charges $55/year per shed (https://www.vmsa.org.au/memberships/).

**The important structural point,** which multiple shed pages state: a large part of that
$60 is insurance. The membership fee is not priced against the value of the workshop, it is
priced against the cost of covering the member. That is a defensible, easily explained basis
for a fee at a place where people use tools and heat, and The Harvest will face exactly the
same insurance question once members are in a sauna. I did not find a shed publishing the
insurance split as a line item, so the "large part" claim is **inferred** from repeated
mentions rather than verified from an audited figure.

### 8. Nottingham Hackspace (UK): the documented walk-back

The most instructive failure in this set, and the founder wrote it up himself.

From 2010 the fee was set entirely by the member. "Since the hackspace was founded in 2010,
membership fees have always been set by the member, whatever you feel the hackspace is worth,
is should be what you pay. The minimum was effectively GBP 0.01, and in quite a few cases,
that's what members paid, whether they used the space a lot or not."

What broke it: "in 2021, we accidentally went over our VAT threshold and incurred significant
costs." A minimum of GBP 5 per month (approx A$9.50) was announced at the 2022 AGM and
implemented on 1 October 2023.

The result, in their own numbers: 170 members were emailed on 2 October, and on 17 October
"148 memberships were revoked. This brought our membership numbers down from roughly 650 to
500 over night." Those 148 members had been contributing about GBP 250 per month between them,
which is about GBP 1.69 each.

Their honest post-mortem on the comms: "admittedly, we could have been clearer on what 'soon'
meant". And on reaction: "The majority of responses have been very polite and positive... There
were some fairly impolite responses though, too... At the time of writing, we've had zero
responses informing us that they cannot afford the increase."

Their recommended, non-binding rate remains GBP 15/month (approx A$29) "for a semi-regular
user of the space (e.g. once or twice a month). This is based on the costs to run the space
over the number of members we have."

Source: https://nottinghack.org.uk/minimum-membership-fees/ (posted 24 October 2023)

**Three lessons.**
1. A free or near-free membership tier produces a large roster that is not a paying group.
   23% of the membership was paying under GBP 5 a month. This is the map's stated tension,
   observed in the wild.
2. Fixing it costs roughly a quarter of the membership overnight, and almost nobody who left
   said affordability was the reason. They just stopped bothering.
3. The trigger was tax, not principle. A VAT threshold breach. The Harvest's equivalent
   question, GST registration on recurring subscription revenue and which entity receives it,
   is already flagged in the map and is not a detail to leave until later.

### 9. Swansea Hackspace (UK): member-hours versus public-hours

The cleanest published example of that boundary.

**Price.** "Membership is on a 'Pay what you can, but pay monthly' basis, we recommend a rate
of GBP 15 a month paid by standing order or direct debit, with a minimum of GBP 5 a month
suggested for those with restricted income." (approx A$29 and A$9.50) "If you have difficulty
in committing to such payments but would still like to be a member and contribute then please
do discuss it with one of our directors."

**What is gated.** Unlimited access to the space (24-hour access for keyholders), discounted
or free workshops, use of all tools, project storage, voting rights, discounted components.

**What stays open to anyone.** One sentence does the whole job:

> "Attendance at the regular open nights is without charge and open to all."

And the counterpart, which keeps the paid tier honest:

> "Attendance at workshops, or use of the space without a membership subscription may attract
> additional fees."

**Storage is priced separately and by size,** which is how they ration a genuinely finite
resource without argument: box GBP 10/month, small locker GBP 20/month, medium locker
GBP 25/month (approx A$19, A$38, A$48). Members supply their own padlock.

Source: https://swansea.hackspace.org.uk/Main/Membership (page last edited 20 January 2026)

Bristol Hackspace runs a similar "the amount you pay is your choice relative to the amount of
use you get from the space" model with a suggested GBP 15/month, and York Hackspace suggests
GBP 30/month with concessions. Both are **secondary** here; the Bristol wiki returned empty to
my fetch and I did not open the York wiki directly.

### 10. School Farm CSA, Dartington, Devon (UK)

The best-written sliding scale I found anywhere. A small organic market garden supplying veg
shares to two towns and the farm gate.

**Four self-selected price bands for the same product.** Standard share, per month
(2026 figures):

| Band | Price | Approx AUD/month | Their own justification |
|---|---|---|---|
| Subsidised | GBP 69.30 | approx A$132 | "The farm could not sustain selling veg shares at this price alone, but it is possible if others are able to give more. For those who sometimes struggle to pay the bills." |
| Regular | GBP 86.60 | approx A$165 | "This price covers the basic running costs of the garden and paying our growers at the Real Living Wage." |
| Supporter | GBP 104 | approx A$198 | "To put it simply, this price allows someone else to join at the subsidised rate, supporting another household with less financial means to access healthy veg." |
| Investor | GBP 173.20 | approx A$330 | "This option is an investment in the farm and its future. It will enable us to pay our team for more of the hours actually worked, invest in the infrastructure of the farm, and run more community focussed events without reliance on grant funding." |

A small share runs GBP 41.50 / 52 / 62.30 / 104 per month across the same four bands.

Source: https://www.schoolfarmcsa.org.uk/membership

**How the self-assessment is made honest without any means testing.** Two moves. First, they
ask for engagement rather than proof:

> "Our veg shares are priced on a sliding scale, you choose what you pay. This system relies
> on your engagement with our motivations so please take a moment to read the following and to
> self-assess your financial means."

Second, they point to an external tool, The Green Bottle Scale, so the reader has something
objective to check themselves against rather than just a vibe. Third, and this is the part
worth stealing outright, each band is labelled with what the *farm* can do at that price, not
with what the *buyer* gets. The buyer gets the identical box at all four prices. The only
difference is what the money makes possible. That reframes paying more as a choice about the
farm's future rather than a status purchase.

**Presentation.** Price is at the bottom of a long "How it works" page, after seasonality,
share size and collection points. Story leads, unambiguously.

### 11. Stearns Farm CSA, Framingham, Massachusetts (US): compulsory work hours, with a buyout

**Price (2026).** Classic share USD 1,004 plus a USD 15 membership fee (approx A$1,441 plus
A$22). Farmer's Choice share USD 660 plus the same USD 15 (approx A$947).

**The work requirement is not optional, but it is priced.** "Sharers are required to
contribute a minimum number of hours of field labor over the course of the growing season,
half of which must be completed by mid-July. (These hours can be paid for if working in the
fields isn't feasible for you.)" The Classic share carries 6 work hours, the Farmer's Choice
4 hours, and both can be "paid out for $12/hr" (approx A$17).

**How it is tracked without heavy admin.** They run a "Work Hours Sign-up" page and a "Work
Hours FAQ" inside the member account area, so hours are booked as slots in advance rather than
logged retrospectively. Booking a shift is the record. That is the low-admin trick: do not
count hours worked, count shifts claimed.

**A separate, deeper tier.** "A limited number of work-for-shares are available. In return for
making a commitment to work 4 hours/week during the farm season, working sharers receive a
generous share of the harvest." Capped by number, gated by a stated physical requirement
("You must be physically fit for the demands of farm work"), and still requires the USD 15
membership fee.

Source: https://stearnsfarmcsa.org/become-a-sharer/summer-share/

**The design to note.** A USD 15 membership fee sits underneath everything, including the
work-for-share people who pay nothing else. Everyone is a member. What varies is what they
buy on top. That is a clean answer to the map's open question about whether "member" should
stay one word for two relationships: keep the word, make it cheap, and sell the products
separately.

### 12. CAC Beardsley Community Farm, Knoxville, Tennessee (US): pure sweat equity

The simplest labour-for-food formula I found, stated in one line:

> "4 volunteer hours = 1 box of food (share)"

> "Earn a share weekly or monthly, depending on how many hours per week worked."

Minimum four hours per month to stay in. Members must be 18 or older. Limit one application
per household, and "all household members may contribute volunteer hours towards the share",
which quietly solves the household-rate question without a household price. Volunteer sessions
run on fixed days and times, so the farm controls when the labour arrives.

**Capacity is rationed by a waiting list, stated up front:** "WorkShare program has limited
capacity. Please email us or submit an application to be put on our wait list."

Source: https://www.beardsleyfarm.org/workshare-csa

No money changes hands at all. That is the purest form of "membership costs effort", and it
works because the output being traded (a box of vegetables) has a defined unit. Effort-for-
sauna-access has no such natural unit, which is the difficulty The Harvest will hit.

### 13. Sutton Community Farm, London (UK)

Community-owned farm with a veg box business. Their "Community" page is a good example of
presenting four different relationships as four doors rather than four price tiers:

> "Grow good food / Become a volunteer. Eat seasonally / Become a customer. Support the farm /
> Become an owner. Donate to charity / Become a donor."

Member benefits listed are social rather than transactional: seasonal events, and community
lunches every fortnight. Notably the lunches are gated behind volunteering, not payment: "Once
you sign up to volunteer, you'll find all the details on our online rota Three Rings."

Volunteer rota software: Three Rings. Worth noting as a named tool for tracking contribution
without spreadsheets.

Source: https://www.suttoncommunityfarm.org.uk/community

I could not source their current veg box prices or their community share terms from primary
pages within this ticket. **Not verified.**

### 14. Fordhall Community Land Initiative, Shropshire (UK)

A 128-acre organic farm owned by community shareholders since 2006.

**Structure.** Non-profit-making community shares at GBP 50 each, minimum holding GBP 100
(2 shares), approx A$95 per share and A$191 minimum. "A minimum shareholding in the Fordhall
Community Land Initiative is GBP 100 (2x GBP 50 shares), and they mean that you part-own
Fordhall Organic Farm for life."

Source: https://www.fordhallfarm.com/buy-a-share

The original 2006 offer raised roughly GBP 600,000 from over 7,500 people at GBP 50 a share
(**secondary**, via Power to Change and Co-operatives UK case studies:
https://www.powertochange.org.uk/evidence-and-ideas/case-studies/fordhall_farm/). A 2026 share
offer is running for the 20th anniversary, targeting 1,000 new shareholders.

**What a share gets you.** Ownership for life and a say. Not a discount, not access, not a
recurring benefit. There is no annual fee. Two separate legal entities exist: the community
land initiative that owns the land, and Fordhall Farm Ltd, the trading farm business.

**Why this matters.** It is the opposite of a subscription. One payment, no ongoing revenue,
strong belonging. It raises capital, not operating income. For The Harvest, which has a lease
rather than a freehold and a planned Year One loss, this model raises money for a *thing*
(a building, a sauna) and cannot solve running costs.

### 15. The Old Forge, Inverie, Knoydart (Scotland)

Britain's remotest mainland pub, bought by its community. Formed as The Old Forge Community
Benefit Society Ltd by nine local volunteers; keys handed over 28 March 2022. Locals raised
over GBP 320,000 through a community share offer and a Crowdfunder. Serves a resident
community of about 130 people plus thousands of visitors, and "reinvests profits back in to
the business and other community projects".

Source: https://www.theoldforge.co.uk/about

**Their line on the boundary between owner and visitor is the useful one:**

> "Community is at the heart of everything we do here, and you don't have to live on Knoydart
> to be a part of that. Come along, get the craic, and join the pub family."

**I could not find any published shareholder discount, member price or locals rate on their
site.** So the honest reading is: shares confer ownership and a vote, and the pub is priced
the same for everyone. If that is deliberate, it is a strong position, because it means the
paywall and the community claim never collide. **Unverified as a deliberate policy.**

Wider UK context (**secondary**, Plunkett UK): 182 community-owned pubs, over 37,500
shareholders, over GBP 35 million raised; and for village shops, "the face value of shares
might be set at GBP 10 to GBP 20 to allow membership with the purchase of only one share"
(https://plunkett.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/Community-shares.pdf).

### 16. Australian community-owned pubs: Grong Grong, Lockington, Sea Lake

**Secondary throughout.** Reported by Australian Hotelier
(https://theshout.com.au/australian-hotelier/nsw-country-pub-becomes-a-cooperative/) and
related coverage: locals in the Riverina village of Grong Grong, NSW bought The Royal Hotel
via $5,000 shares; the pub has 169 shareholders, "mostly locals and those connected to the
town", and raised more than $1m, double what was expected. The Lockington Community Hotel
Cooperative in northern Victoria took over its pub in 2020, with similar structures at Sea
Lake and Nandaly, plus a general store co-op at Colbinabbin and a post office co-op at
Bridgewater.

I did not verify these from the co-ops' own documents. The number worth carrying forward is
the share price: **$5,000 per share in an Australian farming village**, against GBP 50 at
Fordhall and GBP 10 to 20 for a UK village shop. Australian rural community capital raises
have historically gone deep with few holders rather than wide with many, which produces
ownership concentrated among people who can find $5,000. That is a different community than
a GBP 50 share offer produces, and it is a choice, not an accident.

## Part 2: The recurring models

Six distinct shapes appear across the sixteen. Most organisations run two or three at once.

### Model A: The discount card

Members pay a small annual fee and get a percentage off things that are for sale anyway. The
place itself stays free. Examples: CERES (A$66/yr, 10% off), Northey Street ($15 to $250/yr,
10% off plants), Community Sauna Baths Regulars Club (50% off sessions).

- **Gates:** nothing. Price, not access.
- **Trade-off:** the community claim stays completely intact, because nobody is ever turned
  away. Revenue is modest and only breaks even if members actually spend. The 10% versions
  (CERES, Northey Street) are effectively donations with a courtesy discount attached; the
  50% version (Community Sauna) is a real commercial instrument that needs volume to work.
- **Admin:** near zero. One annual charge, a flag on a contact record, a discount at the till.
- **The catch:** at 10% off, a member needs to spend A$660 a year at CERES to break even on a
  A$66 fee. Most do not, and everyone knows it. That is fine, as long as the page is honest
  that the fee is support, not a deal. CERES is honest about it. Brisbane Tool Library, at
  "less than the cost of one drill", is honest in the other direction.

### Model B: The metered allowance

Membership buys a defined number of uses per period, enforced by booking software. Examples:
Blue Mountains Sauna (2 to 7 sessions/week, A$32 to A$91/week), Community Sauna Baths
Unlimited Off-Peak (unlimited but capped at 2 bookings/day, off-peak only), Brisbane Tool
Library (6 or 12 items per loan).

- **Gates:** the scarce facility, precisely.
- **Trade-off:** this is the only model in the set that reliably solves rationing, because the
  cap *is* the product. It also converts a capacity problem into a pricing ladder, so heavy
  users pay more without anyone having to police anything. The cost is that it feels like a
  gym, and the tier ladder is visibly commercial.
- **Admin:** low, but only if you buy software. Blue Mountains runs on Punchpass, Brisbane
  Tool Library on MyTurn. Neither built anything.
- **Most transferable piece:** the peak / off-peak split. It costs nothing to implement, it
  rations without saying no, and it lets a cheap tier exist without cannibalising the paid one.

### Model C: The bounded free allowance for members, paid beyond

Not fully present as a named model anywhere I looked, but it is the implicit shape of
Community Sauna Baths' NHS tier (GBP 2/month buys two free slots per month plus a cheaper rate
beyond that) and of Beardsley's four-hours-equals-one-box.

- **Gates:** nothing absolutely. It caps the free portion.
- **Trade-off:** it is the honest answer to Ben's stated position in the map. "Members get the
  sauna free" is unaffordable at A$45 a visit. "Members get two sauna sessions a month
  included, then member rate after that" is affordable, is easy to say, and preserves the
  perk. The risk is that it reads as mean unless the included number is generous enough to be
  used, and the cap has to be enforced by the booking system or it will not be enforced at all.
- **Admin:** medium. Requires per-member usage counting, so it needs booking software from day
  one, not later.

### Model D: Sweat equity

Labour substitutes for money. Examples: Beardsley (4 hours = 1 box, minimum 4 hours/month,
waitlisted), Stearns Farm (mandatory 4 to 6 hours per season, buyable out at USD 12/hr, plus a
capped work-for-share tier at 4 hours/week), Sompasauna (users heat the stove themselves).

- **Gates:** varies. At Beardsley it gates the produce entirely; at Stearns it is compulsory
  for everyone regardless of what they paid.
- **Trade-off:** it is the model with the strongest community claim and the weakest revenue.
  It only works where the output has a natural unit (a box of veg, a fired stove). It works
  badly for access to a facility, because there is no obvious exchange rate between an hour of
  weeding and an hour in a sauna, and any rate you publish will be argued with.
- **How the admin stays light, and this is the key finding:** nobody in this set logs hours
  retrospectively. **They book shifts in advance.** Stearns runs a "Work Hours Sign-up" inside
  the member account, Beardsley publishes fixed volunteer sessions on set days, Sutton uses
  Three Rings rota software. The shift booking *is* the record. Counting hours after the fact
  is the thing that turns this model into unpaid administration.
- **Second key finding:** Stearns makes the hours **buyable out at a published rate**
  (USD 12/hr, approx A$17). That single move removes almost all of the enforcement problem.
  Nobody has to chase anyone; the unworked hours simply become an invoice. It also puts a
  defensible number on what an hour of contribution is worth, which is the number The Harvest
  will otherwise have to invent.

### Model E: Member-hours versus public-hours

Some hours belong to everyone, some hours belong to members. Examples: Swansea Hackspace
("Attendance at the regular open nights is without charge and open to all", against 24-hour
keyholder access for members), Community Sauna Baths ("Priority member slots"), Blue Mountains
Sauna (session types: silent sessions, men's, women's, parents and bubs, all on fixed days).

- **Gates:** time, not space.
- **Trade-off:** the strongest model for keeping a community claim credible next to a paywall,
  because there is always a real, named, recurring, free way in. Anyone can point at Thursday
  night. It costs nothing to run and requires no software. The weakness is that it does not
  raise money on its own; it is a companion to another model, not a model in itself.
- **Most transferable:** almost directly. The Harvest already runs work days and community
  meals. Naming one recurring open session that is free forever, and saying so in the same
  breath as the price, does most of the credibility work.

### Model F: One-off ownership

A share bought once, for life, conferring a vote and belonging but no discount and no access.
Examples: Fordhall (GBP 50/share, minimum GBP 100), The Old Forge (community share offer,
GBP 320,000 raised), Grong Grong Royal Hotel ($5,000/share, 169 shareholders).

- **Gates:** nothing at all. Buys governance and a story.
- **Trade-off:** it raises capital, not operating income, and it raises it once. It creates the
  deepest loyalty of any model here (8,000 people call themselves Fordhall's landlords) and it
  never conflicts with the community claim, because nobody gets a better price than anybody
  else. But it will not fund a Year One operating loss, and it comes with a governance
  obligation you cannot unwind later.
- **Relevance to The Harvest:** low right now, high later. The Harvest holds a lease, not a
  freehold, trades through a sole trader pending the Pty Ltd cutover, and has not settled
  governance. A share offer is a thing to do when there is an asset to buy and an entity to
  hold it, not a thing to do to get subscription revenue started.

## Part 3: What looks most transferable, and why

**The combination that recurs at the places most like The Harvest is A + C + E, with a
self-declared price ladder borrowed from School Farm and Northey Street.**

1. **Keep membership cheap, annual, income-banded and self-declared.** Northey Street's ladder
   ($15 health care card / $30 part-time / $60 full-time / $125 supporter) requires no means
   testing and no documentation, because nothing expensive turns on it. CERES does the same
   with two bands. This solves the map's concession question without administration. If a
   concession rate ever gates something genuinely costly, ask for the card then, the way
   Northey Street does for allotments and Community Sauna does for GBP 5 sessions.

2. **Do not make the sauna free to members. Give members a bounded allowance and a member
   rate.** Blue Mountains Sauna establishes that an Australian sauna visit is a A$45 product.
   Community Sauna Baths, a not-for-profit whose entire mission is access, still charges its
   members for every session and merely halves the price. No comparable organisation in this
   set gives its scarce, staffed, capacity-limited facility away free to members. That is the
   single clearest signal in the research.

3. **Name one recurring free session and say so on the pricing page.** Swansea Hackspace's
   sentence is the model. CERES puts "free to visit" in the site header on every page,
   including the membership page. This is what stops the paywall from eating the community
   claim, and it costs nothing.

4. **Price the scarce assets separately from membership.** Northey Street rents allotments;
   Swansea rents lockers by size; Blue Mountains sells private hire. None of these are
   membership perks. The Harvest's sauna, workshop slots and space hire are its first earning
   capability, and folding them into a membership fee removes the revenue before it earns, as
   the map already notes.

5. **If effort is going to earn access, book shifts rather than count hours, and publish a
   buyout rate.** Stearns Farm's USD 12/hr buyout, approx A$17, is the mechanism that makes a
   contribution requirement enforceable without anyone having to enforce it.

6. **Label price bands by what the money does, not by what the buyer gets.** School Farm's
   four bands all deliver the identical box. The difference is described entirely in terms of
   the farm's ability to pay its growers, cover its costs, subsidise a neighbour, or invest.
   This is the presentational move most worth copying, because it makes a higher price a
   contribution rather than a status purchase, and it is the only way found here to run a
   sliding scale without means testing and without resentment.

**And the warning.** Nottingham Hackspace ran a free-to-near-free membership for thirteen
years, discovered it could not fund the place, introduced a GBP 5 minimum, and lost 148 of
roughly 650 members in a fortnight, with almost none citing affordability. The Harvest has
188 free members today. If those members are converted to a paid tier, expect a similar
proportion to fall away, expect it to be about inertia rather than money, and expect the
trigger to be a tax or entity deadline rather than a strategic decision, unless the entity and
GST question is settled first.
